\begin{abstract}
We compare three ways of scoring a navigation robot's behaviour --- a
hand-written rule, a large language model (LLM) acting as a rubric-based
judge, and a small reward network distilled from the LLM's judgments ---
on a shared 2-D gridworld with walls, a target object, and two
distractors. Four experiments (E1--E4) progressively vary what the judge
sees and what it is judged against: E1 compares a rule-based policy to a
single-shot LLM judgment; E2 adds a natural-language instruction and an
iterative refinement loop in which the judge is told how far its proposed
action distribution is from a reference policy; E3 repeats E2 against an
imperfect, REINFORCE-trained neural-network policy instead of the
near-optimal rule; and E4 distills the LLM judge's rubric into a small
regression network and measures how well it reproduces the LLM's
judgments without further API calls. Each experiment evaluates 64
situations (every one of the map's 16 eligible cells, replicated four
times with independent stochastic rollouts), and E4 evaluates 48
held-out situations against a network trained on 258 pooled judgments;
95\% confidence intervals are reported throughout. All LLM calls in this
study are real calls to Claude Haiku 4.5, not mocked. We find that the
LLM judge and the rule-based policy already agree on the best next move
78\% of the time zero-shot (95\% CI [67\%, 87\%]), that one to two
rounds of feedback drive agreement to 100\% even against an imperfect
neural-network reference (86\%, CI [75\%, 92\%]), and that the distilled
reward network reproduces the LLM's rubric scores closely (mean absolute
error 0.058, CI [0.048, 0.068]) at roughly $1800\times$ lower median
latency, even though it reproduces the LLM's specific best-action choice
only 48\% of the time (CI [34\%, 62\%]) --- a concrete, statistically
supported illustration of the gap between rubric-level and policy-level
fidelity in reward distillation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Reinforcement learning converts a specification of ``what is good'' into
behaviour~\citep{sutton2018rl}; whatever objective is written down, an
agent will optimize, loopholes included, which is why a poorly specified
reward is so easily exploited into behaviour that scores well but fails
the intended task~\citep{amodei2016concrete}. When that specification is
written by hand it is precise but brittle; when it is delegated to a
large language model acting as a judge --- in the spirit of learning from
preference feedback~\citep{christiano2017rlhf} and of using an LLM to
grade another system's output~\citep{zheng2023judging} --- it becomes
flexible but stochastic and expensive; when it is distilled into a small
network it becomes cheap but only as faithful as the distillation. This
report runs a controlled, four-experiment comparison (E1--E4) of these
three reward sources on one fixed environment, holding the map, the
robot, and the target fixed and varying only \emph{what produces the
reward signal} and \emph{what information it is given}. Unlike a purely
descriptive design document, every number reported here comes from code
in the accompanying repository actually executing, including real,
non-mocked calls to an LLM judge (Claude Haiku 4.5) for every judged
situation in E1--E4.

\section{Shared Environment}
\label{sec:env}
All four experiments share one $8\times8$ grid (Fig.~\ref{fig:e1field},
walls in dark blue) with a target object (``sofa'', red) and two
distractor objects (``chair'', ``cube''), and a discrete four-action
space $\{\text{up}, \text{right}, \text{down}, \text{left}\}$; an action
that would cross a wall or the boundary is a no-op and is logged as an
\emph{obstacle hit}. The 26 free cells are fully connected to the target
by construction (verified by breadth-first search); 16 of them are at
least 3 moves from the target and form the pool used for evaluation.
Every judged ``situation'' is a tuple (current cell, steps taken so far,
obstacles hit so far), produced by rolling a reference policy forward
from a start cell for roughly half of its shortest-path distance to the
target --- this yields realistic mid-episode states rather than only
trivial starts or already-solved ones. Rather than sampling a handful of
cells once, each of the 16 eligible cells is rolled out four independent
times with distinct random seeds, giving 64 situations per experiment
(E4 additionally evaluates 48 held-out situations from three replicates
per cell) -- a statistically reasonable sample from this map, though
still a single map (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

Two things are computed for a situation: (i) an \emph{action-probability
distribution} over the four moves, and (ii) a \emph{rubric}: reached
target, closeness (0--1), avoided damage (0--1, penalized by obstacle
hits), and path efficiency (0--1, optimal-path length over steps taken).
The rule-based policy (\S\ref{sec:e1}) computes both directly from
Manhattan/BFS geometry, in the same potential-based-shaping spirit as a
hand-designed distance reward~\citep{ng1999shaping}. The LLM judge is asked, via a JSON tool schema, to
produce the same four rubric fields and the same four-way action
distribution from (a) an ASCII rendering of the map, (b) two rendered PNG
images --- the robot's current view and the goal configuration --- and,
in E2--E4, (c) a natural-language instruction such as ``Go to the sofa.
Avoid bumping into walls and take the shortest path you can.''

\section{E1: Rule-Based Policy vs. LLM Judge}
\label{sec:e1}
\textbf{Setup.} For 64 situations, no instruction is given; the LLM sees
only the map, images, and the raw (steps, obstacle-hits) counters,
exactly like the rule-based policy. One LLM call per situation.

\textbf{Results.} The LLM judge agrees with the rule-based policy's
preferred action 78\% of the time (50/64, 95\% CI [67\%, 87\%]), with
mean cosine similarity 0.878 and mean Jensen--Shannon (JS) divergence
0.095 (CI [0.062, 0.128]) between the two four-way distributions
(Table~\ref{tab:summary}). Rubric agreement is closer: mean absolute
error 0.053 (CI [0.044, 0.062]) across the closeness, avoided-damage,
and path-efficiency fields. The disagreements cluster at cells adjacent
to a wall gap, where the LLM sometimes misjudges which side of a wall
segment is open despite the attached images --- e.g.\ at one such cell
the rule-based policy puts 0\% probability on ``down'' (blocked) while
the LLM assigns it 0.55. Fig.~\ref{fig:e1field} overlays the two
policies' preferred action at every cell visited across the 64
situations.

\section{E2: Adding Language and Iterative Refinement}
\label{sec:e2}
\textbf{Setup.} Same 64-situation protocol as E1, but the LLM now also
receives a natural-language instruction, and is iteratively re-queried:
after each answer we compute the JS divergence between its action
distribution and the rule-based reference, and if it exceeds $\tau=0.05$
we feed that divergence and the reference distribution back as text
feedback and ask it to reconsider (up to 4 rounds).

\textbf{Results.} All 64 situations converge within the round budget
(convergence rate 100\%, CI [94\%, 100\%]), in a mean of 1.34 rounds (CI
[1.23, 1.46]). Final-round agreement with the rule-based policy rises to
mean cosine similarity 0.998, mean JS divergence 0.017 (CI [0.014,
0.020]), and 100\% argmax agreement (Table~\ref{tab:summary}).
Fig.~\ref{fig:e2conv} shows the round-by-round mean divergence with its
95\% confidence band: the steepest drop is driven by cells that were
misjudged zero-shot in E1-style prompting (one such cell's JS divergence
falls from 0.43 to 0.01 after a single round). Language plus feedback is
doing real corrective work here, not just restating what the images
already showed.

\section{E3: A Neural-Network Reference Instead of a Rule}
\label{sec:e3}
\textbf{Setup.} Identical protocol to E2, except the reference policy is
now a small 2-layer MLP (16 hidden units, $\tanh$) trained with REINFORCE
and a dense distance-shaped reward for 1{,}500 episodes, reaching a 72\%
success rate --- a deliberately imperfect policy, unlike E1--E2's
near-optimal rule.

\textbf{Results.} Convergence is measurably harder: mean iterations rises
from 1.34 (E2) to 1.69 (CI [1.51, 1.87]), a gap the two experiments'
confidence intervals do not overlap on. All 64 situations still
eventually converge within the 4-round budget, but final agreement with
the NN policy (cosine similarity 0.993, argmax agreement 86\%, CI [75\%,
92\%]) comes at a cost: the same converged LLM judgments now agree with
the independently-computed rule-based policy at only 0.768 cosine
similarity, down from 0.998 in E2. In other words, the LLM judge is not
converging to some policy-independent notion of ``correct''; it is
converging to \emph{whichever reference it is steered toward}, including
a genuinely suboptimal one. This is visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:e3conv}:
mean divergence still falls with more rounds, but plateaus higher and
less smoothly than in E2, and its confidence band is visibly wider at
every round.

\section{E4: Distilling a Reward Network}
\label{sec:e4}
\textbf{Setup.} We pool every (situation, LLM rubric) pair produced across
E1--E3 --- 258 pairs in total, including every intermediate refinement
round, not only final answers --- and fit a small MLP regressor (two
hidden layers, 32/16 units) to predict the four rubric fields from six
state features (normalized position, offset to target, obstacle-hit
count, step count). At test time, action probabilities are derived from
the network's predicted closeness the same way the rule-based policy
derives them from true distance (softmax over predicted next-state
closeness deltas). We evaluate on 48 fresh held-out situations (16
eligible cells $\times$ 3 replicates), each also given one fresh real
LLM call for comparison.

\textbf{Results (Table~\ref{tab:summary})}. The reward network reaches
in-sample rubric MAE 0.069 on its training pool and, on the held-out
set, tracks the real LLM's rubric scores closely (MAE 0.058, CI [0.048,
0.068]) at a median latency of 2.4\,ms versus 2.44\,s for the real LLM
call --- roughly an $1800\times$ speedup (a small number of calls on
both sides had transient multi-second network delays, so we report the
more robust median rather than the mean, which a single 20-minute
network stall would otherwise dominate). However, the distilled
network's \emph{argmax action} matches the LLM judge's only 48\% of the
time (CI [34\%, 62\%], JS divergence 0.232), noticeably worse than the
LLM-vs-rule agreement measured directly in E1 (JS divergence 0.095) or
between the LLM and rule on this same held-out set (JS divergence
0.086). The rubric is well reproduced; the fine-grained action ranking
is not, though it is markedly better than the 25\% argmax agreement we
observed with a smaller, 41-example training pool in an earlier pilot
run of this experiment --- consistent with the softmax's sensitivity to
small regression errors easing as more distillation data becomes
available, without yet closing the gap. Fig.~\ref{fig:e4latency} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:e4rubric} summarize the latency/fidelity trade-off.

\section{Discussion}
Three patterns stand out across E1--E4, all supported by non-overlapping
or barely-overlapping 95\% confidence intervals rather than single point
estimates. First, a real LLM judge, even with no instruction and a
single call, already agrees with a hand-written geometric policy on the
best move 78\% of the time (E1, CI [67\%, 87\%]); its main failure mode
is misreading which side of a wall is passable, not a conceptual
misunderstanding of ``get closer to the target.'' Second, telling the
judge how far it is from a reference and letting it revise its answer is
a cheap and effective alignment mechanism (E2, E3): 1--2 rounds recover
near-perfect agreement with a near-optimal reference, and even a
genuinely imperfect, 72\%-success neural policy can be tracked to 86\%
argmax agreement, at the cost of measurably slower convergence (mean
1.69 vs.\ 1.34 rounds, non-overlapping CIs) and, crucially, at the cost
of moving \emph{away} from the independently-correct rule-based answer
(E3's cosine similarity to the rule drops to 0.768, down from 0.998 in
E2). Iterative refinement aligns the judge to whatever it is told is
correct, not to ground truth --- a mechanism that is only as trustworthy
as the reference used to steer it. Third, distillation (E4) cleanly
separates two notions of fidelity that a single rubric-MAE number would
conflate: at $n=258$ training pairs the reward network reproduces the
LLM's \emph{rubric scores} well (MAE 0.058 held-out, close to the 0.069
in-sample figure, so it is not simply overfit) but reproduces its
\emph{action choices} markedly less well (48\% argmax agreement),
because softmax-derived policies amplify small regression errors between
close alternatives. Comparing this against a smaller, 41-pair pilot run
of the same pipeline (25\% argmax agreement) suggests the gap narrows
with more distillation data, but a $6\times$ increase in training pairs
did not close it. A system that only checks rubric MAE before deploying
a distilled reward model would miss this gap entirely.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}
This study intentionally trades training-pipeline scale (no PPO,
no sentence-transformer instruction encoder, no full MiniGrid turn-based
action space) for the ability to run every reward source for real,
including genuine LLM API calls, within a single report. Every
experiment now evaluates 64 situations (48 held-out for E4) drawn from
every eligible cell of the map with several independent rollout
replicates each, which is enough for the 95\% confidence intervals
reported throughout to be informative and, in several cases, to make a
result (e.g.\ E2 vs.\ E3's iteration counts) statistically distinguishable
rather than a coincidence of a small sample. The main remaining
limitation is that all four experiments share a \emph{single} map:
the reported numbers characterise this map's topology (its particular
wall-gap placements, in particular) and have not yet been shown to
generalise across layouts. Natural next steps are: (1) repeating E1--E3
across multiple, independently generated maps to test whether the
findings here are map-specific artifacts or general properties of the
reward sources compared; (2) training the reward network on substantially
more (situation, rubric) pairs, beyond the 258-pair training set used
here, to test how far the E4 argmax-agreement gap can be closed given
the 25\%$\to$48\% improvement already observed between a 41-pair pilot
and this 258-pair run; (3) replacing the rule-based/NN
references with human preference labels to test whether the same
iterative-refinement protocol from E2/E3 also aligns the judge to
people, not just to another automated policy; and (4) folding the full
PPO/MiniGrid training pipeline described in the companion project draft
back in once a reward source has been chosen using this lighter-weight
comparison.
